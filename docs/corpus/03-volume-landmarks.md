# Volume landmarks: how much work is the right amount

## Counting work: what a hard set is

I count a set as work when it was taken at RPE 7 or above on a movement that trains the pattern I am counting it for. Warm-ups do not count, however many of them there are. Speed work at RPE 5 does not count. A set of curls does not count toward the squat, no matter how much the lifter enjoyed it.

Counting sets is a crude unit and I use it anyway. A set of five squats at RPE 9 and a set of twelve leg presses at RPE 8 are not the same stimulus, and no unit I have tried fixes that. Tonnage is worse, because it rewards adding reps to light weights. Sets are cheap to count, stable week to week, and precise enough to notice that a lifter went from eleven hard squat sets to nineteen in a fortnight, which is the kind of thing worth noticing.

## Minimum, maximum, and the useful middle

There is a floor below which the training holds what you have and adds nothing. For a competing lifter on the squat pattern, I have found that floor sits around six to eight hard sets a week. Below it, strength holds for a month or two and then slides.

There is also a ceiling, and it is not the point where training feels hard. It is the point where you stop recovering from it, which shows up a week later. In a moderate-intensity squat block my own ceiling lands near eighteen to twenty hard sets a week. In a block where working sets sit at RPE 8.5 for triples, the same body tolerates closer to twelve.

Between the two is the band where the work pays. It stays a band rather than a number because both edges move with everything below.

## Why the landmarks move

Intensity moves them most. Heavier sets cost more per set, so the ceiling drops as the block gets sharper. A lifter who thrived on twenty sets in accumulation will bury themselves on sixteen during intensification.

Training age moves them slowly and upward. Someone with five years of consistent training can absorb work that would flatten them in year one, both because they recover better and because their sets are more efficient.

Life moves them without warning. A new job, a newborn, a bad month of sleep, and the ceiling that held all last year is now too high. The volume did not change. The recovery available to pay for it did.

## Reading your own signals

Volume is too low when sessions feel comfortable, you leave the gym fresh most days, and the log shows loads at a given RPE flat for three or four weeks with no other explanation.

Volume is too high when performance at a fixed RPE drifts down across the week. Ordinary fatigue means Friday is harder than Monday and next Monday is fine. Too much volume means next Monday is worse than this Monday, and the Monday after that is worse again. Joints starting to complain before muscles do is another early one.

Judge on the week, not the session. A single session can be ruined by four hours of sleep or a bad warm-up. Four sessions across a week pointing the same direction is a trend you can act on.
