# Periodising supportive equipment

## What each piece of equipment does

A belt gives your abdominals something to brace against, which raises trunk stiffness and lets you keep position under a load that would fold you without it. Most lifters find twenty to thirty kilos on a squat between beltless and belted once they have learned to use one, and learning to use one takes weeks.

Knee sleeves are warmth, a little compression, and a small amount of rebound out of the bottom of a squat. Knee wraps are a different tool entirely: stiff canvas wrapped tight enough to store real energy, worth substantially more than sleeves on a squat and painful enough that nobody wears them for warm-ups. Wrist wraps stop the wrist extending under a bench press or an overhead load, which keeps the bar stacked over the forearm.

The competition categories follow the gear. Raw means lifting without supportive equipment. Classic, which is what most federations run and what most people mean when they say raw, allows a belt, wrist wraps and knee sleeves. Equipped adds squat suits, bench shirts and knee wraps. Check your own federation's list before a meet, because the line between these categories moves between federations and between years.

## Training without the gear on purpose

I keep part of every accumulation block below the equipment the lifter competes in. Beltless squats and pulls for the backoff work, sleeves off for lighter sessions, wraps nowhere near the training floor until late.

What that builds is the trunk and the positions the gear otherwise does for you. A lifter who has worn a belt on every set since their first year has a brace that only works with a belt on, and it shows the moment position gets challenged. It also gives you somewhere to go, since a lifter already in full kit every session has spent their equipment before they needed it.

Never training bare leaves ungeared strength far behind the geared numbers, which turns every taper into a gamble on the gear covering the gap.

## Bringing the gear in toward a peak

Equipment comes in as the loads come up, and in order of how much it changes the lift. The belt first, once working sets pass roughly RPE 8. Sleeves and wrist wraps next. Knee wraps last, in the final three or four weeks, because they punish the knees and take practice to apply consistently.

Before the meet, the lifter needs sessions in the exact competition setup: same belt on the same notch, same wraps, same shoes, same order of putting them on. The last heavy session of the peak is a full dress rehearsal, because a new belt that digs into your ribs should reveal that in training and not on your second attempt.

Loads from geared and ungeared work do not compare directly. A belted, wrapped squat can be fifteen percent above the same lifter's beltless squat, so an e1RM built from beltless backoff sets and one built from wrapped top sets are two different numbers about two different lifts. I keep them apart and I never let one prescribe loads for the other.

## Equipment during a deload

The gear comes off during a deload, or most of it does. Loads are down to RPE 6, so the equipment is not needed, and a week of beltless work at moderate loads is a cheap way to keep the brace honest. Wraps in particular have no place in a deload, since the joints are meant to be recovering.

Carry the fit details from week to week: belt notch, sleeve size, how the wraps are applied. Reset what the loads assume. A setup logged in a peak week should not silently follow the lifter into a deload and make the reduced loads look lighter than they were.
