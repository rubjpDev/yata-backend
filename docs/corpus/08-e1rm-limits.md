# What an estimated 1RM can and cannot tell you

## What an estimate is doing

An estimated 1RM takes a set you did complete and infers the single you did not. Five reps at 150 kg with two reps left in the tank implies a heavier single than five reps at 150 with five left, and the estimate turns that relationship into a number.

It is useful because a true max is expensive. Testing a real single costs a session, produces days of fatigue, and can only be done a handful of times a year without eating the training around it. Meanwhile you finish twenty working sets a week, each one carrying information about your current strength. The estimate reads that information so you can prescribe next week's loads from this week's training instead of from a max you tested in March.

## Where the estimate degrades

Rep range is the first thing that ruins it. An estimate from a triple or a set of five is close enough to plan with. An estimate from a set of twelve is a guess wearing a decimal point, because everything that separates lifters at one rep, tolerance for a long grind, breathing, how fast fatigue accumulates within the set, has had twelve reps to compound.

Lifters differ from each other, and the same lifter differs across the three lifts. Some people hold near-maximal loads for reps and estimate low against their true single. Others have a big single and fall apart at five, so their estimates read high. In my experience the deadlift is the least reliable of the three, since a heavy pull sits closest to a genuine one-rep effort and the drop from three reps to one is steeper than it is on the squat.

An inaccurate RPE feeds straight through. The estimate is built on the reported reps in reserve, so a lifter who habitually reports a 9.5 set as an 8 will produce estimates several percent above their real strength, and every load prescribed from those estimates will arrive too heavy. Nothing downstream can detect this. The number looks the same whether it was honest or not.

## Trends beat single numbers

Any one estimate carries the noise of one set on one day: the warm-up, the sleep, whether the lifter guessed their RIR well. The best estimate across recent sessions smooths most of that out, which is why I read the last few weeks rather than today's figure.

A rising trend across three or four weeks means the block is working, whatever any individual session felt like. A flat trend across three sessions on a plan that should be adding weight means fatigue has caught up, and it is one of the first things I check before deciding on a deload. A falling trend means it caught up a while ago.

## What you do not use it for

Meet attempts do not come off an estimate. They come off what the lifter has actually handled in the final weeks, in competition equipment, at competition commands, with the opener set at a weight they could hit on their worst day. An estimate has never been paused, never been pressed on a signal, and never been done in front of three judges.

Comparing your estimate to someone else's is meaningless, because their number encodes their rep tolerance and their honesty about RPE, neither of which you can see.

An estimate is a tool for prescribing loads within your own training. A tested max is a fact about what you lifted. The estimate is how you decide what to put on the bar on Tuesday, and it should never be written in the column where a real result goes.
