# When to deload, and what a deload actually is

## Signals that a deload is due

The log shows it before the lifter feels it. Three sessions in a row where the best estimated 1RM on a lift fails to move, on a plan that was supposed to move it, is my first criterion. Flat means flat within noise, so a gain under about half a percent counts as no gain.

The second criterion is RPE creep at a fixed load. When the same weight for the same reps costs a full RPE point more than it did two or three weeks ago, the lifter is paying more for the same work. That gap is the cleanest fatigue signal I have found, and it usually arrives a week before anyone complains.

The third is time at the top of the volume band. Two consecutive weeks at the highest volume the lifter tolerates is enough to justify a deload whether or not the other signals showed up.

Subjective signals are real but I do not act on them alone. Motivation dips on Tuesdays. Sleep gets blamed for sessions that were badly programmed. When a lifter tells me they feel wrecked and the log agrees, we deload. When they feel wrecked and the log shows three weeks of steady progress, we look at the warm-up first.

One bad session is a bad session. The signals above all describe a direction held across weeks, which is what separates a trend from a Wednesday.

## Planned versus reactive deloads

I schedule one into the block in advance, usually after four to six weeks depending on how heavy the block runs. Planning it means the lifter arrives at it intact instead of broken.

I pull it forward when the signals arrive early, and that happens often enough that the scheduled date is a ceiling, not a promise.

The costs are not symmetrical. Taking a deload you did not strictly need costs you a week of slightly reduced work, and you come back sharp. Skipping one you did need costs two to four weeks of training that produces nothing, plus whatever injury turns up while you are grinding heavy singles on a body that stopped recovering.

## What you actually cut

I cut volume first, usually to half the working sets, and hold the loads near where they were. That keeps the lifter feeling strong while the fatigue clears. When joints are the complaint rather than general tiredness, I cut intensity instead and keep some volume, taking working sets down to RPE 6 for the same rep ranges. When both are true, both come down.

What stays is the frequency, the movement patterns, and the technique. The lifter still squats, benches and pulls on the same days.

A deload is training. A rest week is not. Take seven days off and you lose the skill groove on the competition lifts and come back to a bar that feels foreign, which costs you the first sessions of the next block relearning what you already knew.

## Coming out of a deload

The deload worked when loads that felt heavy the week before feel ordinary again, and the first working set of the new block lands at or below its target RPE.

I bring the lifter back at about ninety to ninety-five percent of the loads they left, not straight back to where they stopped. The week you stopped at was the week you were most fatigued and least accurate about your own effort, so the loads from it are the loads you trust least.
