# Autoregulating training with RPE

## What RPE measures

RPE rates the set you finished, not the weight you loaded. Two lifters can both squat 180 kg for 5 and report a 7 and a 9.5, and both can be telling the truth about their own set.

When I call a set RPE 8, I mean the last rep slowed down but never stalled, my position held, and I believed I had two more reps of that same quality in me. At RPE 9 the last rep is visibly slower than the one before it and I am not sure about a second. At RPE 10 the bar stops moving before I decide to stop.

The same 180 kg can be a 7 on Tuesday and a 9 on Saturday. Nothing changed on the bar. What changed is the lifter carrying it: sleep, food, the previous session, whether the warm-up felt like work.

## Why a percentage of 1RM is not enough on its own

A percentage describes a load you once lifted, under conditions you will not repeat exactly. Five hours of sleep, an argument, a skipped lunch, or a 7 a.m. session instead of 7 p.m. all move what today's bar feels like, and none of them show up in the spreadsheet.

Holding a fixed percentage through a bad week costs twice. The first sessions turn into near-maximal grinders logged as moderate work, technique degrades under a load you are no longer in charge of, and by Friday the accumulated fatigue eats the block you were building. The lifter is training hard and getting worse.

Percentages still earn their place in planning. I use them to lay out the volume of a block, to set a starting load for the first session, and to sanity-check that a lifter's numbers are moving over months. I do not use them to judge whether a single day went well.

## How autoregulation changes a prescribed session

If the first work set comes in above the target RPE, I take load off. Roughly 3 to 5% for one RPE point of overshoot, then the next set tells me whether the correction was enough. If it comes in below target, I add load in the same size steps, but never more than two upward adjustments in one session, because a set that felt easy at rep 3 can still bury you at rep 8.

The adjustments have a floor. If I have dropped more than 10% off the prescribed load and I am still above target, I stop chasing the number, cut the remaining sets to the reduced load, and let the session be a light day. Trying to rescue a bad day inside the session is how a bad day becomes a bad week.

## Reporting RPE honestly

Motivated lifters under-report. The set that was a 9.5 gets logged as an 8 because admitting otherwise means the next session gets lighter. Tired lifters do the opposite and inflate the number to justify stopping.

A number that is consistently one point wrong poisons everything built on top of it. Load progression, volume decisions, deload timing, and this app's suggestions all read the same log. If the log says 8 when the set was a 10, the system will keep adding weight to a lifter who is already failing.
