# Organising training into blocks

## Why blocks instead of a single continuous plan

A block gives one quality the priority for a few weeks so it has room to change. Chase size, maximal strength and peak expression in the same week and each one gets a third of your recovery, which is rarely enough to move any of them. The lifter trains hard for months and their numbers sit still.

My blocks run four to six weeks, and what decides the length is how fast the lifter accumulates fatigue rather than the calendar. A lifter who reaches the top of their volume band in week three is running a four-week block whether the plan said so or not.

The intents I plan with are accumulation, intensification, peak, deload, and general for the stretches with no competition in view.

## Accumulation

Accumulation buys work capacity and muscle. Volume is the highest it will be in the cycle and the loads are the lightest, because the point is to do a lot of quality reps rather than a few heavy ones.

Working sets sit around five reps at RPE 7, with backoff sets of eight at RPE 6.5. That leaves three reps in reserve on the hardest sets, which is what makes twenty sets a week survivable. Sets climb across the block while the RPE targets stay put, so the load tracks the lifter rather than the plan.

It worked if the same RPE now costs more weight than it did in week one, and if the lifter reaches the end of the block tired but still adding reps.

## Intensification and peaking

Intensification trades volume for load. Working sets drop to triples at RPE 8.5 with backoffs of five at RPE 7, and total sets come down by a third or more, because sets at 8.5 cost far more than sets at 7. The lifter feels stronger here even though the weekly work is lower.

Peaking is shorter and narrower still. Doubles at RPE 9 with light backoffs, only the competition lifts, and only enough of them to stay sharp. The peak is not where strength is built. It is where you express what the previous twelve weeks built, and where you rehearse the singles, the commands and the equipment you will meet on the platform.

You cannot hold a peak because the thing that makes it work is low fatigue on top of high fitness, and low fatigue is exactly what heavy singles destroy. Two to three weeks is what I plan for. Past that the loads keep climbing on paper while the lifter gets worse.

## Sequencing blocks across a training year

Each block sets up the next. Accumulation builds the tissue and work capacity that intensification converts into strength, and intensification builds the strength that a peak expresses. Run them the other way round and the peak has less to express.

A deload sits between blocks as a matter of course, and gets pulled forward inside a block when the log asks for it. I would rather start intensification a week late and fresh than on schedule and flat.

With no meet on the calendar, I run general blocks: longer accumulation, more variation work, weak points attacked while there is time to attack them, and no peaking at all. The off-season is where you fix the thing that limited your last meet, because there is no room to fix it in the eight weeks before the next one.
