# RPE and RIR: two ways to say the same thing

## RIR: counting what is left

RIR is a count of reps in reserve. When the set ends you answer one question: how many more reps did you believe you had at that quality? If the honest answer is three, the set was RIR 3.

Counting what remains is easier than rating effort because it asks about reps, and reps are a unit you already work in. "How hard was that, out of ten" invites you to compare today's set against every hard set you have ever done, including one from four years ago that you remember wrong. "How many more could I have done" keeps the question inside the set you finished thirty seconds ago.

## The relationship between the two scales

Subtract the reps in reserve from ten and you have the RPE. RIR 3 is RPE 7, RIR 2 is RPE 8, RIR 1 is RPE 9. That arithmetic holds well enough between one and four reps in reserve, which covers almost all the work worth logging.

It falls apart at the top. The gap between "one left" and "none left" is the hardest call in the whole system, because you are being asked to predict a rep you did not attempt. A lifter who reports RIR 1 has claimed knowledge about a rep that might have failed halfway up. I trust RIR 2 and 3 far more than I trust RIR 0 and 1, and I build programs on the range I trust.

It also thins out at the bottom. Below RPE 6 the number stops carrying information. A set with six reps left is a warm-up, and calling it 4.5 instead of 5 changes nothing about what you do next.

## Which one to log, and staying consistent

Pick one and stay with it. Which one matters less than whether the meaning of a 7 in January is the same as the meaning of a 7 in June.

When the scale changes silently mid-block, the training history stops being comparable to itself. Say you spent six weeks reporting RPE by subtracting reps in reserve, then drifted into reporting how hard the set felt overall. The numbers in the log look continuous. They are not. Every load progression, every e1RM estimate, and every deload decision built on those weeks is now reading two different measurements as one series, and nothing in the data announces the switch.

## Where both scales are unreliable

Accuracy degrades as the reps go up. On a set of five I can usually call the reserve within one. On a set of fifteen the last five reps all feel terminal, and lifters routinely report RIR 2 on a set they could have carried four more reps. High-rep accessory work gets an RPE in my logs, but I never let it drive a load decision.

Newer lifters report low. Someone in their first year will call a set RIR 2 when they had five in the tank, because they have never been to failure and do not know what the edge feels like. This corrects with exposure, not with instruction.

Grinders and technical breakdown tell you what the number cannot. If the bar slowed to a crawl, or the back rounded, or the knees caved on the last rep, the set was harder than whatever got written down. I log the number the lifter gives me and I coach off what I saw.
